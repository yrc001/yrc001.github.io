While touring open houses in Sunnyvale last weekend, I overheard a conversation between homebuyers and a real estate agent about local "toxic sites" (Superfund sites).

They were discussing where these sites are, whether they still pose health risks, and how to check if a home is built on contaminated land.

As someone who works with spatial data and lives in the area, I decided to look into it. I quickly found that the EPA provides detailed environmental data, including contamination records and groundwater plume maps.

But this raised a question for me: **If this information is publicly available, how easy is it for ordinary homebuyers to find and understand?**

When people make decisions about where to live, do they have access to reliable environmental data, or do they still depend mostly on word of mouth?

---

## A Brief History: Silicon Valley's Semiconductor Industry

Silicon Valley grew rapidly with the semiconductor industry, especially between the 1960s and 1980s. Companies in Mountain View, Sunnyvale, and Santa Clara used industrial chemicals to manufacture computer chips.

Two common chemicals were **TCE (trichloroethylene)** and **PCE (tetrachloroethylene)**, which were used to clean and degrease silicon wafers.

Over time, leaks from industrial facilities and underground storage tanks contaminated soil and groundwater.

Nobody drinks that groundwater today, and these areas remain under long-term environmental monitoring. The main concern in some residential areas is **vapor intrusion**—which happens when chemicals in contaminated soil or groundwater turn into gas and move into buildings through cracks or other openings.

---

### What is a Superfund Site?

A "Superfund site" is what people usually refer to as a **toxic site**. 

Back in 1980, Congress established the **Superfund program** to give the EPA the funds and authority to clean up the country's most severely contaminated hazardous waste sites. 

When a site gets added to the **National Priorities List (NPL)**, it becomes an official Superfund site, meaning the EPA steps in to monitor and clean it up (using remediation systems like soil vapor extraction).

## Where to Find Superfund Maps

The EPA provides several tools for finding information about contaminated sites.

### 1. EPA Superfund Site Search

You can start with the [EPA Superfund Site Search](https://www.epa.gov/superfund/search-superfund-sites-where-you-live).

The website provides all site information, technical reports, and location details for contaminated areas in the US.

However,  **It only shows a single dot (a point location), not the actual boundary.** (as shown in Figure 1).

If you want to see actual boundaries instead of just pins, try another EPA tool called [Cleanup in My Community](https://www.epa.gov/cleanups/cleanups-my-community). 

Just type in your ZIP code, and it lets you see mapped boundaries and plumes (as shown in Figure 2).
